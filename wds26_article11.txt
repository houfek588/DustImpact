Dust particle impact detection on charged space probes in the solar wind
P. Houfek, J. Souèek, and O. Santolík
Charles University Prague, Faculty of Mathematics and Physics, Prague, Czech Republic.
Institute of Atmospheric Physics, Czech Academy of Sciences
Abstract. This paper presents a numerical model designed to simulate dust particle impacts on the surface of a charged spacecraft within the solar wind environment. The modelling process is divided into two stages. In the first stage, the steady-state electrostatic potential around the spacecraft, resulting from interactions with the surrounding plasma, is calculated using the SPIS software. The second stage utilizes these intermediate results as input conditions for a custom computational script that simulates the expansion of the plasma cloud generated by the dust impact. The model tracks the dynamics of electrons and ions in the vicinity of the spacecraft and calculates the resulting voltage response on the measurement antennas. The results, presented in a 3D approximation, show characteristic signal waveforms and facilitate a better interpretation of data from real space missions.
Introduction
    Measuring the properties of interplanetary dust particles (IDPs) in the inner heliosphere is a key task for modern space missions, as these particles carry valuable information about the formation and evolution of the Solar System (Mann et al. 2019). However, traditional dedicated dust impact detectors suffer from a fundamental limitation: due to their small dimensions, often with an effective area of <0.1 m^2, they yield a very low statistical capture rate under low dust fluxes.
    An important step in IDP research occurred with the discovery that standard electric dipole and monopole antennas, primarily designed for wave and plasma measurements, can be utilized as highly sensitive, non-conventional dust detectors. Examples include the Radio and Plasma Waves (RPW) instrument on the Solar Orbiter spacecraft or the FIELDS instrument on Parker Solar Probe (Zaslavsky et al. 2012; Mann et al. 2019). In this non-conventional detection method, the entire conducting main body of the spacecraft with an effective area of several square meters serves as dust detector.
    The physical principle of detection is based on impact ionization. Upon the impact of a dust grain at hypervelocity v>10 km/s, its kinetic energy is instantaneously converted into heat, leading to rapid vaporization and ionization of both the projectile and the spacecraft's surface shield material (Zaslavsky 2015). This generates an expanding, highly dense plasma cloud composed of free electrons and ions , a phenomenon extensively documented in early laboratory frameworks (Friichtenicht 1962; Drapatz a Michel 1974) and later confirmed by in-situ wave antenna instruments on pioneer space missions (Gurnett et al. 1983; Meyer-Vernet et al. 2009). The rapid expansion of lightweight electrons and the slower movement of heavier ions, known as ambipolar expansion, lead to a spatial separation of charge, which in turn induces transient voltage pulses on the spacecraft's measurement antennas (Shen, Sternovsky, Garzelli, et al. 2021; Shen et al. 2023).
    Interpreting these signals is extremely challenging due to the high variability of the pulse shape, polarity, and amplitude, which are connected to the local asymmetry of spacecraft charging and the influence of the photoelectron sheath in the solar wind (Koèišèák et al. 2024). Existing models face a severe multiscale problem. The inability to simultaneously simulate both the global steady-state charging of the spacecraft on scales of seconds and the transient nanosecond expansion of the dense impact-generated cloud.
    This work addresses this scientific gap by presenting a two-stage hybrid 3D PIC (Particle-in-cell) framework that successfully integrates steady-state 3D electrostatic fields from the specialized SPIS (Spacecraft-Plasma Interaction Software) tool into a custom, time-optimized 3D PIC solver.

Model of measurement antennas
    The resulting voltage on the antenna V_ant is obtained by solving a first-order ordinary differential equation describing a parallel RC circuit (Zaslavsky 2015; Koèišèák et al. 2024):
    (dV_(ant,a))/dt=(I_(ind,a)+I_(col,a))/C-(V_(ant,a)-V_(eq,a))/RC
    where I_col is the current of particles physically collected by the voxel mask of the antenna, C is the parasitic capacitance of the antenna system, R is the effective dynamic sheath resistance, and V_eq is the equilibrium floating potential of the antenna in the quiet solar wind before the dust impact. 
    Voxel represents a discrete values on a regular, three-dimensional rectilinear grid. In the context of this framework, the spacecraft's complex 3D geometry is discretized into these volumetric elements to form a binary spatial mask, allowing the Monte Carlo particle-boundary collision algorithm to efficiently determine whether a macroparticle has intersected the physical structure of a specific antenna.
Two-stage methodology and SPIS integration
    The success of the simulation relies on the hybrid coupling of two distinct software architectures.
Global steady-state charging in SPIS 3D
    In the solar wind environment, the Solar Orbiter spacecraft is exposed to an intense flux of electrons, ions, and solar UV radiation, which generates a massive photoemission current from sunlit surface elements (Guillemant et al. 2012).     On this grid, a voxel mask is created, assigning a unique identifier to each node [i,j,k] which could be free space, spacecraft main body, RPW antenna 1, 2, or 3.
Transient 3D PIC solver with dynamical coupling
    The custom PIC solver solves the equations of motion for particles and updates the field in each time step ?t. The discretization of the 3D Laplacian is performed using a standard 7-point central difference equation.
    ?^2 V_(i,j,k)?(V_(i+1,j,k)-2V_(i,j,k)+V_(i-1,j,k))/(?x^2 )+(V_(i,j+1,k)-2V_(i,j,k)+V_(i,j-1,k))/(?y^2 )+(V_(i,j,k+1)-2V_(i,j,k)+V_(i,j,k-1))/(?z^2 )
    Since the grid geometry remains constant, the system matrix does not change over time. The solver performs LU matrix factorization only once during initialization. In each time step, the new field (under a changing V_ant (t)) is computed via a fast forward and backward substitution.
    The computed antenna voltage V_ant (t) from the RC equation immediately modifies the boundary conditions on the antennas, recalculating the 3D background field E ?_bg (r ? ) and thus dynamically affecting the motion of the moving particles in the next time step. Altogether, this is creates self-consistent loop.
2D Poisson-Boltzmann model as a proof of concept
    During the early phases of framework development, a simplified 2D PIC solver was designed, in which the background plasma shielding is approximated by the linearized Poisson-Boltzmann equation:
    ?^2 V_bg-1/(?_D^2 ) V_bg=0
    This simplified 2D model implements the shielding term directly onto the diagonal of the system matrix as a coefficient of -1/(?_D^2 ).
    In the development cycle, this 2D PB version serves as an effective and fast proof of concept / sanity check to debug the numerical stability of the RC feedback, the interaction of the Ramo-Shockley integrators, and particle detection via the voxel mask. From a physical standpoint, however, the 2D model is insufficient for comprehensive analyses, as it neglects the spatial asymmetric character of the Debye sheath, the ion wake, and the spatial vector nature of induction on deployed antennas. The core innovation of this work therefore lies exclusively in the full 3D hybrid coupling with SPIS.

Spatiotemporal evolution of the 3D electrostatic potential

Figure 3: 3D animation of the spatial evolution of the total electrostatic potential V ?_total (r ? ) around the Solar Orbiter spacecraft and its antennas after a hypervelocity dust particle impact. Impact place is marked by red circle.
    In the initial state prior to the dust impact t<t_delay, the electrostatic environment in the vicinity of the spacecraft is characterized by a stable, shielded potential structure directly imported from the SPIS 3D simulation. At the moment of impact t=t_delay, a transient phase is initiated by the release of a dense, highly localized plasma cloud, where the rapidly expanding electrons immediately disrupt the background electrostatic equilibrium, having an impact as a negative potential drop that propagates from the impact location. This expansion triggers a dynamic feedback loop. The moving charges induce variable currents, the resulting fluctuating voltage V_ant (t) on antennas is dynamically projected back at each numerical time step as updated Dirichlet boundary conditions on the spatial antenna masks. This creates a characteristic motion and spatial oscillation of the equipotential lines surrounding the RPW antennas.
Kinetics of the plasma cloud expansion

Figure 4: 3D kinetic animation of macroparticle positions of electrons (red) and aluminium ions ?Al?^+ (blue) expanding into 3D half-space from the impact location on the Solar Orbiter spacecraft surface.
    The expansion dynamics exhibit a clear ambipolar separation between the two plasma species immediately following the hypervelocity impact. Driven by a high initial thermal velocity v_(th,e)?8.4?10^5  m/s at T_dust=2 eV, the lightweight electrons (red) rapidly decouple from the heavier core to expand as an isotropic, spherical cloud. The spacecraft structure carries a positive floating charge accumulated from solar wind interactions and photoemission, so a significant fraction of this expanding electron population is instantaneously recollected by the nearby conductive surface elements, yielding a sharp, transient negative current spike.
    In contrast, the ion population (blue) is consisting of heavy aluminium ions ?Al?^+ (Koèišèák et al. 2024; Shen et al. 2023). It propagates orders of magnitude slower v_(th,i)?3.8?10^3  m/s than the electron shell. Governed by a flux-weighted Rayleigh distribution for the forward velocity component v_x at the boundary interface, their kinetic emission naturally conforms to Lambert's cosine law rather than expanding symmetrically. This causes the ions to assemble into a directional, cohesive cloud that propagates perpendicularly away from the impact wall, generating a prolonged inductive signature as the expanding plasma cloud passes the RPW antennas.
Waveform analysis of currents and voltages on RPW Antennas

Figure 5: Time series of simulated induced/collected currents (top panel) and the resulting voltages V_ant (t) on the RC filters of the three RPW antennas (bottom panel) after a dust grain impact at t=1.0 ?s.
    Immediately following the dust impact at t=1 ?s, a rapid and sharp negative current pulse is detected across all antennas. This primary transient is driven by the immediate recollection of high-velocity electrons from the expanding plasma cloud, combined with initial electrostatic induction. As the electron cloud disperses and leaves antenna area, the total registered current shifts toward positive values. The resulting voltage profiles V_ant (t) document a highly non-uniform and asymmetrical response among the individual sensors, establishing that the Radio and Plasma Waves (RPW) instrument does not register the impact event uniformly.
    This spatial discrepancy is dictated by the specific geometric proximity of each antenna relative to the initial impact site. Positioned in close proximity to the localized expansion. Antenna 1 registers the earliest and most dominant negative voltage step due to rapid electron collection. This primary negative peak is immediately followed by a positive overshoot driven by ion induction, which undergoes a series of minor spatial oscillations before entering a steady relaxation phase. Located furthest from the impact zone and partially shielded by the spacecraft's conducting main body, Antenna 2 records a significantly attenuated negative peak, exhibiting the longest propagation time delay across the array and transitioning directly into exponential relaxation with an almost negligible positive overshoot. Antenna 3, situated at an intermediate spatial vector, registers a negative peak slightly larger in amplitude than that of Antenna 1, albeit with a distinct time delay of approximately 4-5 ?s corresponding to the finite transit time of the electron cloud. Following this delayed negative maximum, the signal relaxes smoothly back toward baseline without positive overshoot.
    Once the main electron cloud is absorbed or leave the calculation area, the voltage across all three antennas decays exponentially back to their respective equilibrium floating potentials V_eq. In Figure 5 this can be observed in times t>5.0 ?s. This RC discharge phase is governed by the local plasma sheath conditions, yielding a characteristic time constant of ?=RC?0.2 ?s. The distinct variations and spatial asymmetries reproduced by the model match real waveforms qualitatively, confirming that the combination of direct particle catch, localized spatial induction, and spacecraft body shielding represents the primary source of waveform variability observed in flight data (Koèišèák et al. 2024).
Discussion and future outlook
    The presented two-stage hybrid 3D PIC framework functions as a robust numerical proof-of-concept, yet it remains an active development model requiring further microphysical and circuit-level refinements to achieve full quantitative convergence with inflight datasets. Future iterations must address several coupled plasma-surface and electrical phenomena currently simplified within the core solver.
    The physical interaction between the expanding cloud and the conductive hardware boundaries is highly complex. High-velocity electrons and ions impacting the metal surfaces of the antennas can trigger secondary electron emission SEE. The process influences the net collected current I_col and can locally change the localized sheath polarity (Wang et al. 2015; Champion a Schaub 2023). Integrating an energy and angular dependent SEE yield model across the voxelized 3D boundaries of the RPW antennas is important to correctly resolving the structural amplitude variations observed in secondary voltage peaks. This plasma-surface interface is further modulated by radiation effects; at the perihelion distances of Solar Orbiter (<0.3 AU), the extreme solar extreme-ultraviolet EUV flux can cause rapid photoionization of the expanding neutral gas component that failed to ionize during the initial shock. This secondary ionization acts as a continuous volumetric source of cold electrons and ions, change the net charge balance and can cause long duration tails in the waveforms.
    Space for development lies also in model of the basic electrical architecture of the spacecraft. The current model simplifies the system into isolated, independent parallel RC circuits connecting each antenna directly to a generalized environment. In reality, the complex geometry of Solar Orbiter could demand a fully coupled multi-node equivalent circuit model that incorporates a complete mutual capacitance matrix, accounting for the cross-talk and capacitive coupling between individual antennas and the massive main body. Implementing a self-consistent circuit solver capable of updating a spacecraft potential in sync with the 3D PIC field updates could be important for get response which is closer to the hardware response.
    Finally, the model currently assumes an unmagnetized plasma domain. In accordance with laboratory and theoretical work by Nouzák (Nouzák et al. 2020), neglecting the ambient interplanetary magnetic field B ? is physically justified for the transient microsecond intervals and 5 m spatial limits considered here, as the characteristic Larmor radii of the heavy aluminium ions remain vastly larger than the simulation domain, thereby rendering the Lorentz force v ?×B ? negligible over the primary signal duration.
Conclusion
    We have presented two-stage hybrid 3D PIC framework for modelling the transient electrostatic phenomena associated with dust impacts on the surface of the charged Solar Orbiter spacecraft. The proposed methodology of separating the steady state in SPIS and the transient PIC computation has proven to be numerically efficient and stable.
    Although the model is still in an active stage of development and requires the implementation of collisional phenomena and secondary emission from the antennas, it already shows qualitative agreement with trends, such as spatial detection asymmetry and waveform shapes, observed by real spacecraft instruments. This model represents a solid foundation for future semi-empirical calibration tools that will enable more accurate interpretation of spacecraft data and estimation of interplanetary dust flux in the heliosphere.
